A genetic answer that nobody can act on

Genetics plays a central role in psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions. But knowing that someone carries a risk gene tells a doctor almost nothing about whether — or how badly — that person will actually be affected. Here is how large that gap is, and why it has stayed open.

Most psychiatric and neurodevelopmental conditions — autism, schizophrenia, major depression — are only diagnosed once symptoms have become severe. By that point, years of schooling, relationships, work and brain development have already passed without support.

The European picture

The burden is just as heavy close to home. Mental ill health affects more than one in five people across EU and OECD countries, making it one of Europe's largest health and economic challenges. Anxiety, depressive and alcohol use disorders alone are projected to cut healthy life expectancy by 2.5 years between 2025 and 2050, absorb roughly 6% of total EU health spending, and cost around 1.7% of GDP every year.

Other European sources fill in the picture: years lived with disability from mental disorders rose by about 31% between 2019 and 2021, mental disorders are now the leading cause of death among people aged 15 to 29 in Europe — largely through suicide — and an estimated 235,000 deaths a year across Europe are linked to mental and behavioural disorders.

The blind spot: incomplete penetrance

In plain terms

Penetrance is simply the chance that a person carrying a particular mutation actually develops the condition linked to it. When that chance is well below 100% — which is usually the case in brain conditions — doctors call it incomplete penetrance. It is the reason a genetic result so often raises more questions than it answers.

Two people can carry the very same mutation and live completely different lives. One develops profound disability. The other never has a symptom. That is not a rare curiosity — it is the norm in neurodevelopmental genetics, and it is one of the most clinically costly blind spots in modern medicine.

Neurodevelopmental conditions, including autism, affect roughly 1 in 100 people worldwide and carry an estimated annual economic burden of more than €250 billion in the EU alone. A defining feature of these conditions is exactly this variability: people carrying identical mutations — for example in the Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signalling pathway, a master regulator of how the forebrain is built — can end up with dramatically different outcomes.

New evidence, 2026

A study published in the European Journal of Human Genetics re-calculated penetrance for recurrent neurodevelopmental copy-number variants and found many published figures had been substantially inflated. Estimates previously reported in the 10–40% range dropped to 1–10%, and eleven variants previously treated as low-penetrance came out close to 0% for intellectual disability.

That is a striking result, and it cuts to the heart of the problem: the numbers currently used to counsel families are derived from DNA statistics, not from measuring what a variant actually does in that person's own cells.

Three failures that compound each other

  • There is no human-relevant way to predict penetrance. Animal models cannot reproduce the variability that makes human brain development distinctive, so genomic results stay clinically uninterpretable for the families and doctors who receive them.
  • There is no biological way to sort patients for clinical trials. Companies cannot tell in advance which variant carriers will respond to a treatment — a major reason trial failure rates in brain disorders run above 90%.
  • There is no scalable platform to do this work. Academic multi-omics studies are one-off and non-standardised. They cannot be run at the volume or consistency that diagnostic labs and pharma partners need.
Two people. The same mutation. One develops the disorder — the other never does. Genetics alone cannot tell you which.

A related problem that others are already chasing

There is a second, adjacent need: predicting which drug will work for a patient who has already been diagnosed. Roughly 63% of people with major depression try several medications before finding one that helps, and about a third still do not respond after two rounds of treatment. That is a real and valuable problem — and it is where most commercial competitors have gone first.

It is not, however, the same problem. Choosing a drug for a diagnosed patient and predicting whether an at-risk person will become ill at all are different questions requiring different biology.
