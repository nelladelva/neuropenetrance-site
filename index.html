Genetics plays a central role in psychiatric and neurodevelopmental disorders — but carrying a risk variant tells clinicians almost nothing about whether, or how severely, a person will actually develop disease. This is incomplete penetrance: two people with the identical mutation can have completely different outcomes.

Neurodevelopmental disorders affect roughly 1 in 100 people worldwide and cost the EU more than €250 billion a year. Yet clinicians, genetic counselors, and drug developers have no molecular tool that predicts which variant carriers will be affected — and clinical trials for CNS drugs fail at rates above 90%, largely because trial cohorts can't be stratified by biological risk.

Two people. The same mutation. One develops the disorder — the other never does. Genetics alone can't tell you which.
The idea

Think of it like a weather forecast — for mental health risk

A forecast doesn't claim it will definitely rain. It reads the signals in the atmosphere and reports a probability you can act on. NeuroPenetrance applies the same logic to biology: instead of a yes-or-no genetic diagnosis, it quantifies risk from molecular data and hands clinicians and researchers a signal they can actually use.

The platform

Two engines, one flywheel

A wet-lab profiling service and an AI biomarker platform, built on the same scientific foundation and designed to strengthen each other over time.

ENGINE 1

iPSC Penetrance Profiling Service

Patient-derived hiPSC lines, differentiated into disease-relevant neuronal subtypes using protocols validated in the Hammes Lab at MDC, and profiled with deep multi-omics and functional readouts.

  • Penetrance Risk ProfileQuantitative molecular signature of genetic vulnerability vs. resilience.
  • Therapeutic Sensitivity ReportPharmacological modulation readouts identifying a patient's response window.
  • Biomarker PanelA ranked set of molecular targets for clinical follow-up or trial enrollment.
ENGINE 2

PenetranSci AI Biomarker Platform

A SaaS interface for pharma and diagnostic partners, built on classifiers trained on multi-omics data — surfacing the strongest biological predictors of disease risk, filterable by pathway, cell type, and developmental stage.

  • Interpretable outputRanked candidate biomarkers, not an unexplained score.
  • Compounding data advantageEvery Engine 1 contract improves Engine 2's models.
  • Scalable deliveryGrows without proportional wet-lab cost.

Every Engine 1 service contract generates proprietary training data that sharpens Engine 2's predictions — which in turn increases the value of every new service contract. It's a compounding advantage that a generic iPSC contract lab or an academic core facility can't easily replicate.

The scientific rationale

Why genetics, genetic modifiers, and proteomics are valid risk signals

This isn't a novel claim — it's the direction the published literature already points. Click through the evidence base behind each layer of the platform.

Genome-wide association studies have linked thousands of variants to psychiatric disorders. Individually, each carries only a small effect — but aggregated into a polygenic risk score, they form a reproducible signal already used for research-grade risk stratification. Recent appraisals frame near-term clinical translation as chiefly a validation and communication challenge, not a scientific one.

Clinical utility of polygenic risk scores: a critical 2023 appraisal Koch et al. · Journal of Community Genetics, 2023 Polygenic risk score as clinical utility in psychiatry: a clinical viewpoint Fujii et al. · Journal of Human Genetics, 2020

Carrying a pathogenic variant is not the same as developing disease. A growing body of literature shows that genetic modifiers — secondary variants that enhance or suppress a primary mutation's effect — along with epigenetic and environmental factors, are a major reason penetrance is incomplete in neurogenetic and neurodevelopmental disorders. That gap is precisely what NeuroPenetrance is built to quantify.

Incomplete penetrance in neurogenetic disorders: current insights and emerging perspectives ScienceDirect / PubMed, 2026 Penetrance, variable expressivity and monogenic neurodevelopmental disorders Isidor et al., 2024

No clinical guideline currently includes a blood-based test for depression — diagnosis still relies entirely on symptom interviews. Proteomic profiling of blood is the leading candidate to close that gap: a decade of studies has identified reproducible candidate protein signatures, and researchers increasingly frame large-scale validation, not discovery, as the remaining barrier to clinical use.

Proteomic approaches to identify blood-based biomarkers for depression and bipolar disorders Expert Review of Proteomics, 2018 Serum proteomic analysis uncovers novel serum biomarkers for depression Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2024
